Automatic colour sorter with a Geneva drive

A machine that sorts parts by colour. A conveyor feeds them onto a platform that turns in exact 90° steps thanks to a Geneva drive. At each stop a sensor reads the colour and three servos push each part into its bin.

The challenge

Coordinating a continuous feed (the conveyor) with a colour reading that needs the part to stand still, followed by physical sorting. The answer was a classic mechanism: a Geneva drive turns the motor's continuous rotation into intermittent 90° steps, and during each pause the platform is mechanically locked.

How it works

  • A conveyor driven by a geared DC motor, with LEGO parts, drops each part at the reading station.
  • A TCS3200 sensor reads the colour inside an opaque housing that shields it from ambient light.
  • A stepper motor (A3967 driver) turns the drive wheel. Each turn rotates the platform 90° and keeps it locked for the remaining 270°.
  • The Arduino tracks the colour of the part at each station and fires the HS-422 servo at the matching one.
  • An LCD shows the detected colour and a count per colour. There is a start/stop button and an emergency stop.

Design and manufacturing

All custom parts were modelled parametrically in SOLIDWORKS following design-for-manufacturing criteria and printed in PLA: the drive wheel, the Geneva wheel, the top platform, the frame that holds the sensor and the servos, and the chutes that take each part to its bin. Before printing, we checked the assembly with interference detection and a motion study so the servo arms would not hit the sensor mount.

Control

The C++ program synchronises three things: the sensor reading, the platform rotation and the servo actuation. To keep any task from blocking the others, the stepper is timed with millis() instead of fixed delays. The sensor is calibrated with reference parts of each colour before use.

  • The conveyor runs and drops a part at the reading station. The sensor reads it and the display shows the detected colour.
  • The conveyor stops, so no other part falls during the move, and the platform advances 90°.
  • The program shifts its four-station register by one position. This way the colour of each part travels in memory while the part turns on the platform.
  • If the colour stored for a station matches its bin, that station's servo pushes the part, returns to rest and the counter for that colour goes up by one. Several servos can act at once if needed.

Reading the colour

The parts are red, green and blue Sugus sweets. The TCS3200 does not return a colour but one signal for each of its filters (red, green and blue). The program measures the three channels and keeps the dominant one. If the reading is too weak or the three channels come out almost equal, it takes the station as empty and stores nothing. The bins sit 90°, 180° and 270° from the reading station: red, green and blue, in that order.

My role

I coordinated the team of four because I was the one involved in most parts of the project. I wrote all the code and did all the electronic wiring, and I printed the parts, which we designed together as a team. I took part in every phase, from the proposal to the final prototype and the poster.
